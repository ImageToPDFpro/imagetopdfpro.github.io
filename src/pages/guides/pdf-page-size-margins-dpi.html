Most of the time you can add images, press convert and be done. But when a PDF will be printed, submitted to an institution, or needs to look polished, the page settings matter. This guide explains page size, orientation, margins, image fit and DPI in plain English, so you can choose with confidence.

Page sizes: A4, Letter and friends

Two paper standards dominate the world. The ISO A series (A4, A3, A5) is used almost everywhere, including Europe, Asia, Africa, Australia and Latin America. US Letter and US Legal are used in the United States, Canada, Mexico and the Philippines.

Which should you choose? Use the size that the recipient's printers use. If you are unsure and you live outside North America, pick A4. The two are close enough that a PDF in either size will print acceptably on the other, with slightly different margins.

"Same as image"

This option makes every page exactly the shape of its image, with no paper size at all. It's ideal for screenshots, infographics, artwork and photo albums viewed on screen. It's less suited to printing, because printers will scale each page to fit the paper.

Orientation

  • Auto (recommended) makes wide images landscape pages and tall images portrait pages, so each image uses the page as fully as possible.
  • Portrait forces every page to be upright, which suits documents that will be bound or stapled.
  • Landscape forces every page to be sideways, which suits slide decks, spreadsheets and wide charts.

Orientation doesn't rotate your image. If a photo itself is sideways, use the rotate button on its card.

Margins

Margins are the white border between the image and the edge of the page.

  • None places the image right up to the edge. Best for on-screen viewing.
  • Small (about 7 mm or 0.28 in) is a clean default that avoids most printers' unprintable edge area.
  • Large (about 14 mm or 0.56 in) leaves room for binding, hole punches or handwritten notes.

Why printers cut off edges: most home and office printers can't print the outer 3–5 mm of the paper. If a document will be printed, avoid None, or important content at the edges may be lost.

Image fit

When an image's shape doesn't match the page's shape, something has to give:

  • Fit whole image scales the image until it touches the page edges (or margins) on two sides. Nothing is cropped, but you may see white space on the other two sides.
  • Fill page scales the image until the page is fully covered. There's no white space, but the parts that overflow are trimmed.

For documents, always use Fit whole image: you never want part of a form or signature cut off. For photos where a little trimming doesn't matter, Fill page looks more polished.

The preview on each card updates instantly when you change these settings, so you can see the result before converting.

DPI: how much resolution do you need?

DPI (dots per inch) describes how many image pixels fit into one inch of paper. The same image looks sharper on a small page than on a large one, because its pixels are packed more tightly.

To work out the DPI of an image on a page, divide its pixel width by the page width in inches. For example, a 2480-pixel-wide image on an A4 page (8.27 in) is 2480 ÷ 8.27 ≈ 300 DPI.

Frequently asked questions

What is a PDF "point"?

PDFs measure pages in points, where 72 points equal one inch. An A4 page is 595 × 842 points. You don't need to work with points directly; the converter handles it for you.

Why does my image look small in the middle of the page?

Images always scale up or down to fit the page, so this usually means the margin is set to Large or the image's shape is very different from the page. Try Orientation: Auto or Page size: Same as image.

Can I use a custom paper size?

Choose Same as image and crop your images to the proportions you need before converting. Each page will match its image exactly.
